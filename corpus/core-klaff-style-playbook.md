# Oren Klaff Style Playbook (core)

This is the always-loaded core of the Klaff corpus. It distills the ideas from Oren Klaff's cold outreach course, *Pitch Anything* and *Flip the Script* into working rules for writing LinkedIn and email replies. It is written in our own words: apply the ideas, never quote or name the books in a reply.

## The three things every outreach message lives or dies on

From the cold outreach course: a cold message works when it is (1) socially connected, (2) relevant, and (3) sent from a position of status.

- **Social connection.** The prospect needs to feel the message came through some thread they recognize, not off a purchased list. Even a light touch counts: a mutual connection, a shared group, a conversation with someone in their world, something they posted. In a reply, keep that thread alive by referencing what they actually said and the world they live in.
- **Relevance.** The prospect must get, within a sentence, that the sender understands their business. Specifics prove it: a term from their industry, a pattern in their market, a problem people in their seat are dealing with right now.
- **Status.** People only listen to someone they believe can affect their own standing. Status comes from calm, from fluency in their world, from being selective, and from not needing the deal. It is lost by chasing, explaining, over-thanking and pleading.

## Frames: whoever holds the frame sets the terms

Every exchange is a collision of frames (the lens each side uses to define what is going on). The stronger, calmer frame absorbs the weaker one.

- **Power frame** (the prospect acting busy, dismissive, or in charge): don't react to it, don't defend against it. Step past it with something unexpected, dry humor, or a small denial that shows the sender isn't rattled.
- **Time frame** ("I've only got a minute", "not now"): never rush to fit their window. Shrink the ask, or take the time pressure off entirely and let them name when.
- **Analyst frame** ("send me details", "what's the ROI", "how does it work"): don't drown them in facts. Facts invite scrutiny. Answer with one sharp specific plus a reason the interesting part is better shown than written.
- **Prize frame**: the sender is the prize, not the prospect. The sender is selective about who they work with. This is the default stance in every reply.
- **Moral authority / intrigue frame**: tell a short, specific story or observation from the prospect's world that makes them lean in because they want to know how it ends.

## The croc brain: why replies must be simple, novel and non-threatening

The first filter in a prospect's head is primitive. It ignores anything boring, rejects anything complicated or threatening, and summarizes everything into a snap judgment. So:

- Keep it short and concrete. If it needs explaining, it's too complex.
- Novelty earns attention: say something they haven't heard from the last ten vendors.
- Never threaten: no pressure, no guilt, no "just following up again".
- Create tension, then release it: push-pull, a takeaway paired with a small step toward them.

## Prizing and push-pull

- **Prizing**: reverse the usual dynamic so the prospect is qualifying themselves to the sender. Ask what they'd need to see, whether they're the right fit, how they currently handle the problem.
- **Push-pull**: pair a step toward them with a step back. "This might not be a fit for you" lowers resistance more than any pitch. A gentle takeaway makes people want what's being withdrawn.
- **No neediness**: neediness is the single biggest status killer. It shows up as over-explaining, thanking, apologizing for the intrusion, repeating the ask, or accepting any time they offer.

## Intrigue and open loops

- Hint at something specific the sender has noticed (a pattern, a gap, something most people in their seat get wrong) and leave the payoff unsaid.
- The open loop should be closable only by a reply or a call.
- Observations are framed as patterns ("what I keep seeing is..."), never invented statistics.

## Flip the Script: let the buyer arrive at the idea

- People trust conclusions they reach themselves far more than conclusions they're handed. Lay out the pieces and let the prospect connect them.
- Signal certainty without pushing: the sender has done this before and knows how it goes.
- Lower the stakes of saying yes. A small, casual next step is easier than a big commitment.
- Treat objections as information about what the prospect actually cares about, not as obstacles to argue down.

## Time constraints and next steps

- Real scarcity only: the sender's calendar is genuinely limited, and they're selective. Never invent deadlines or fake scarcity.
- When asking for time, make it feel like nothing and vary the phrasing. Don't default to "15 minutes" every time. Offer two concrete windows, or let them choose.
- When a call is the goal, pick the reason the call is worth it for *them* (seeing the targeting, a specific idea about their book, a pattern in their market), not for the sender.

## The core problem to avoid: sounding like a bot

Prospects smell a template in the first four words. These tells lower the sender's status:

- **Acknowledging before answering**: "Totally get it", "Makes sense", "Fair enough", "Great question", "Appreciate you getting back to me", "Thanks for the reply". A real person just answers. Cut the opener.
- **Repeating their words back**: if they said "we're all set with marketing", don't write "Sounds like you're all set with marketing". Respond to what they meant, not what they wrote.
- **Summarizing the offer**: one specific detail beats a description.
- **Salesy stock phrases**: "Worth a quick 15 min?", "Would love to connect", "circle back", "touch base", "no worries at all", "I'd be happy to", "just wanted to", "hope this helps", "value add".
- **Perfect symmetry**: every reply shaped acknowledge, reframe, question, ask. Vary the shape.
- **Padding**: every sentence must carry a specific. If it could appear in any outbound message to anyone, cut it.

## Sounding human

- Match their register: mirror punctuation, length and energy. Lowercase and terse from them means loosen up.
- Write like you talk: contractions, fragments, plain words. Starting with "And", "But", "Ha" or nothing at all is fine. Dry humor yes; enthusiasm no. No exclamation points unless they used them. No emojis unless they did.
- Respond to the subtext. "Send me info" usually means "I'm not sure this is worth my time". "We have someone" usually means "I'm not unhappy enough to change".
- Read-aloud test: if a line would sound strange said across a table ("I appreciate you sharing that"), cut it.

## Building depth into a reply

Use two or three of these per reply, not all of them:

1. A reframe that steps past the objection without arguing with it.
2. An insider observation in the prospect's own lingo.
3. An open loop that hints at something more specific behind it.
4. A question they have to think about to answer, ideally about their own numbers, pipeline or clients.
5. A light, optional next step, or none if the question is doing the work.

Length: on LinkedIn, 2 to 4 short sentences, roughly 25 to 60 words; shorter usually wins. Email can run 60 to 120. One idea per reply, and at most one or two industry terms. If the prospect wrote one terse line, answer in one or two. A flat "no" or "stop messaging me" gets one graceful line.

## Reading the objection

| What they say | What it usually means | Move |
|---|---|---|
| Flat "not interested" | Reflexive no | One easy, gracious line that leaves the door open. No re-pitch. |
| Soft "not interested" / "not right now" | Hasn't seen a reason yet | Pattern interrupt, an insider observation, one open loop |
| "Send me info" | Polite stall | A question that gets them talking, plus why the interesting part won't fit in a PDF |
| "We already have someone" | Not in pain | Don't touch the incumbent; poke at a gap they may not have thought about |
| "Too busy" | Not a priority | Shrink the ask, plant one specific hook, let them name a later time |
| "What does it cost?" | Interested, testing | Straight answer or "depends on X", plus the variable that makes talking faster |
| "How'd you find me?" / skeptical | Guarded | Plain, honest, a little disarming; then show relevance with one detail from their world |
| "Reach out next quarter" | Real timing or a soft no | Agree easily, let them set the date, plant a timing hook tied to their calendar |
| Interested but vague | Ready | Skip the dance, give one reason it's worth their time, go straight to the time |

## Industry lingo banks

Use the vocabulary of the *prospect's* world, one or two terms per reply, used precisely and casually, never explained, never stacked. **Match the country.** Accounts, tax rules and regulators differ by country: a Canadian prospect has never heard of QSBS or a 401(k), and a US prospect doesn't have a TFSA. Use the bank for the client's (and prospect's) country.

**Financial advisors / RIAs / wealth teams (US)**: AUM, net new assets, households, $2M+ liquid, ideal client profile, COI relationships, CPA and estate attorney referrals, referral drought, book of business, breakaway, wirehouse vs. independent, compliance pre-approval, pre-retirees, rollover, pension lump-sum decision, liquidity event, concentrated stock position, RSUs and ISOs, vesting cliff, 10b5-1 plan, QSBS, exit planning, tax-loss harvesting, Roth conversion window.

**M&A advisors, bankers, business brokers**: sell-side / buy-side mandates, deal flow, proprietary deals, LOI, IOI, quality of earnings, EBITDA multiple, add-backs, working capital peg, earnout, seller note, rollover equity, platform vs. add-on, lower middle market, founder-owned, succession gap.

**Capital raisers, fund managers, GPs**: LPs, first close, final close, commitments, soft-circled, anchor LP, family office allocations, accredited investors, 506(b) vs. 506(c), capital stack, pref return, carried interest, IRR vs. equity multiple, fund I vs. fund II, re-ups, ticket size.

**Business owners, executives, professionals** (often the prospect when the client is an advisor): exit window, valuation multiple, key-person risk, deferred comp, NQDC, equity grants, double-trigger acceleration, tender offer, concentrated position, partnership buy-in/buyout, practice valuation, succession, tax drag, liquidity event.

**Canada (advisors, business owners, executives)**: RRSP, TFSA, RESP, FHSA, RRIF, holdco, CCPC, lifetime capital gains exemption (LCGE), estate freeze, family trust, IPP (individual pension plan), corporate-class investing, dividend vs. salary mix, CPP/OAS timing, OAS clawback, commuted value of a pension, stock options at a CCPC, CRA, CIRO, IIROC/MFDA legacy, bank-owned dealers vs. independents, book of business, AUM.

**United Kingdom**: ISA, SIPP, pension drawdown, defined benefit transfer, annual allowance, IHT, business relief, BADR (business asset disposal relief), EMI options, growth shares, salary sacrifice, HMRC, FCA, Consumer Duty, IFA vs. restricted adviser.

**US federal employees**: FERS, TSP, high-3, MRA, FEGLI, deferred retirement.

Other niches: pull the equivalent terms from that industry the same way.

## Hard rules

- A clear "no" or "stop messaging me" gets one graceful line. Pressure there burns the client's reputation.
- No invented results, fake scarcity, made-up clients or made-up statistics.
- Financial services clients: no performance promises, nothing that reads as investment advice or a product recommendation. Lingo can describe situations but never promise outcomes.
- Fresh words only. Never lift lines from Klaff's books or from these notes.
